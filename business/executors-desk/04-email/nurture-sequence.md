## Email 1 — day 1
**Subject:** the phone is the key

Before you cancel anything, keep your parent's mobile phone paid for and charged.

Nearly every bank, brokerage and utility sends its security codes to that number. Cancel the line and you lose access to every account behind it, and getting it back means notarized letters and weeks of waiting. The same goes for the email address.

Week-one list, in order:
1. Call the homeowner's insurer. Most policies treat a house as vacant after 30 to 60 days and then void claims. Ask what they need.
2. Keep the utilities on. Do not transfer them yet either.
3. Photograph every room, drawer and the garage before anyone visits. Date-stamped.
4. Lock away cash, jewelry, firearms, medication, documents. Log what you moved.
5. Find the last two tax returns. They list every account that paid your parent money. That is your map.

## Email 2 — day 3
**Subject:** the two words on the law firm's website

"Personally liable." You have probably seen it by now.

It is real, and it is also rarer than the websites make it sound. Executors who end up paying out of their own pocket almost always did one of five things:

1. Paid beneficiaries before the creditor window closed and the taxes were reserved for.
2. Mixed estate money with their own, even briefly.
3. Missed a deadline: filing the will, publishing notice to creditors, filing the inventory, the final tax return.
4. Spent a Social Security or pension deposit that landed after the death. Those get clawed back.
5. Played favorites: the sibling who cleared the garage first, the friend who got the house cheap.

The habit that prevents all five: nothing leaves the estate until you can point to a document that says it can. Say that to your brother when he asks about the truck. Say it kindly, and keep saying it.

## Email 3 — day 6
**Subject:** you might not need probate

Every state has a shortcut for modest estates. It is called a small-estate affidavit or a summary procedure, and the limit runs from about $15,000 to over $200,000 depending on the state, usually counting only the things in your parent's name alone with no beneficiary.

To find out, sort everything into three piles:
A. Passes by beneficiary designation: life insurance, IRAs, 401(k)s, payable-on-death accounts. Not probate.
B. Passes by ownership: joint accounts, a house owned with a surviving spouse, anything in a living trust. Not probate.
C. Everything else. Add this pile up at date-of-death values. That number decides your path.

Then call the probate clerk in the county where your parent lived and ask two questions: what is the small-estate limit this year, and which form. Clerks cannot give legal advice but they answer this every day, and the call is free.
