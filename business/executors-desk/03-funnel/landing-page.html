The tools

This is the part free checklists never include.

  • The "do not do this yet" list. Twelve things people will pressure you to do in the first month, when you are actually allowed to do each, and what goes wrong if you do it early. Tape it to the fridge.
  • Seven phone scripts. The bank before and after Letters. The credit card company. Social Security. Utilities. Life insurers. The homeowner's insurer.
  • Three letters to copy. Credit bureau deceased notice. Notice to a creditor with a date-of-death balance request. Valuation request to a brokerage.
  • Ten trackers. Death certificates. Calls. Mail. Assets. Debts. Recurring bills. Digital accounts. Beneficiaries. Expenses and mileage. A weekly check-in.
  • The state quick reference. Small-estate limits and creditor claim windows for the largest states as starting points, and exactly where to verify them for free.
  • The sibling page. A monthly update email that ends the "what is taking so long" texts, and five fair ways to divide personal things.
  • The lawyer page. When you need one, when you do not, how they charge, and eight questions for the first call.

The five ways executors get personally burned

The notebook opens with these, because they are the point. Paying people too early. Mixing estate money with your own. Missing a deadline you did not know existed. Spending a benefit deposit that gets clawed back. Playing favorites. Each one is a page of plain language and the habit that prevents it.

What it is not

It is not legal advice, and it says so on the cover. Probate is state law and the dollar limits change. The notebook gives you the general shape, tells you where a rule varies, and tells you where to check for free. If your parent's estate involves a business, property in two states, an insolvent estate or a will contest, the notebook will tell you that you need a lawyer, and it will tell you what to ask them.
