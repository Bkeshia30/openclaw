# Executor duties, in order: what to do in the first 90 days

Forty-six percent of people named as executor did not know they had been chosen. If that was you, here is the job, in the order it happens, without the law-firm framing.

## What an executor is, in one paragraph

An executor (in many states, "personal representative"; if there is no will, "administrator") is the person the court authorizes to gather a dead person's assets, pay their valid debts and taxes, and distribute what is left according to the will or state law. You are a fiduciary, which means you must act in the beneficiaries' interest, and you can be personally liable for losses caused by your mistakes. Most executors never face that. The ones who do made one of five avoidable errors, covered below.

## Days 1 to 3

Death certificates (ten to fifteen). The house secured. Dependents and pets handled. The original will located. Nothing thrown away. One folder, one notebook, one person running the phone tree.

## Week one

Homeowner's insurer notified about possible vacancy. Utilities kept on. Every room photographed. Valuables and documents locked away and logged. The last two tax returns found, because every institution that paid your parent money is listed on them.

## Weeks two to four: getting appointed

You have no authority yet. The court grants it with **Letters**. To get them:

1. File the original will with the probate court in the county where your parent lived (many states: within 30 days of death).
2. File the petition for probate. The clerk and the state court's self-help site have the forms. Fees run roughly $50 to $500.
3. Notify heirs and beneficiaries as the court requires.
4. If the will did not waive bond, ask the clerk whether one is required; beneficiaries can often waive it.
5. Receive Letters. Order five to ten certified copies. Institutions want one dated within the last 60 days.

Before filing, check whether you need probate at all. Sort assets into what passes by beneficiary designation, what passes by joint ownership or trust, and what is in your parent's name alone. Only the last pile counts toward your state's small-estate limit.

## The day you have Letters

1. **EIN.** Apply at irs.gov, entity type "Estate." Free, ten minutes. Never use your parent's Social Security number for estate business again.
2. **Estate checking account.** With the EIN and Letters. Every estate dollar in or out goes through it from now on.
3. **IRS Form 56.** Tells the IRS you are the fiduciary so their mail comes to you.
4. **Post office.** Letters and ID in person, PS Form 3575, to forward the mail. A death certificate alone is not enough.

## Days 30 to 90

- Publish notice to creditors in the newspaper the court specifies; keep the affidavit of publication.
- Mail written notice to every known creditor with a request for the date-of-death balance.
- File the inventory with the court by its deadline, often 60 to 90 days after appointment.
- Collect written date-of-death values: bank letters, brokerage statements, a licensed appraisal for real estate.
- Move sole-name accounts into the estate account. Deposit refund checks there. Never into yours.
- Send a written update to all beneficiaries on the same day every month.
- Engage a CPA. The final Form 1040 is due April 15 of the year after death; Form 1041 applies if the estate earns $600 or more after death; Form 706 only if the estate exceeds the federal exemption ($15 million per person in 2026) or to elect portability for a surviving spouse.

## The five mistakes that create personal liability

1. **Paying beneficiaries before creditors and taxes.** If a valid claim arrives after distribution, the shortfall can be yours.
2. **Commingling.** An estate check in your personal account, even for a day.
3. **Missing a deadline you did not know existed.** Filing the will, publishing notice, filing the inventory, the tax returns.
4. **Spending a benefit overpayment.** Social Security does not pay for the month of death; deposits after death are clawed back.
5. **Playing favorites.** The sibling who cleared the garage first; the friend who got the house below market.

The habit that prevents all five: nothing leaves the estate until you can point to a document that says it can.
