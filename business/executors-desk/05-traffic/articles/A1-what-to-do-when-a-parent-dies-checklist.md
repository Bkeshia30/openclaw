# What to do when a parent dies: the checklist, in the order it actually happens

Most checklists for this moment are forty items long, alphabetical, and written by a bank. They are not wrong. They are just useless at 11 p.m. on the second night, when you need to know what has to happen tomorrow and what can wait a month.

This one is in order. It is written for the adult child who has been handed the job, whether or not the will calls you executor.

## The first 72 hours: only what protects people, property and paper

1. **Choose a funeral home and get the legal pronouncement.** The funeral home files the death certificate and, in most cases, notifies Social Security. Ask them to confirm both.
2. **Order 10 to 15 certified death certificates.** Every bank, insurer, brokerage and the DMV wants one, and several will keep it. Copies cost roughly $10 to $25 each depending on the state. Reordering later takes weeks.
3. **Make sure dependents and pets are cared for.** Pets are the most commonly forgotten item on every executor list. Go to the house.
4. **Secure the home.** Lock it, collect spare keys from neighbors and cleaners, bring in the mail, set the thermostat. Keep the address and the service time out of the obituary; empty houses get burgled during funerals.
5. **Find the original will and any trust documents.** Desk, safe, freezer, file cabinet, the attorney's office, the safe-deposit box. Many states require the original to be filed with the probate court within 30 days.
6. **Throw nothing away.** The tax return in that pile of mail lists every bank and brokerage that paid your parent money last year. It is your map.
7. **Check for a prepaid funeral plan or burial insurance before paying for anything.** Keep every receipt regardless; the estate reimburses reasonable funeral costs.
8. **Start one folder and one notebook.** Every call, every name, every date.
9. **Ask one person to run the phone tree.** You cannot field every call and also do this job.
10. **If your parent was a veteran, tell the funeral home.** Burial benefits, a flag, possibly a national cemetery plot.

That is the whole list for the first three days. Everything else waits.

## What not to do this week

People will pressure you to act. Here is what to decline, and why.

| Do not | Because |
|---|---|
| Pay any bill from your own money | Mixing estate and personal money is the most common thing beneficiaries sue over, and some debts are not the estate's to pay at all. |
| Close bank accounts | You lose the paper trail and stop automatic payments (like insurance) you still need. |
| Cancel the mobile phone or email | Nearly every account sends its security codes there. |
| Give anyone the car, the ring, or "their share" | Nothing is distributed until debts and taxes are settled. Early distributions can come out of your pocket. |
| Spend a Social Security or pension deposit that arrived after the death | It will be clawed back. |
| Sign anything a lawyer or collector puts in front of you | You do not need to decide anything this week. |

## Week one: secure and locate

**Call the homeowner's insurer.** Most policies treat a house as "vacant" after 30 to 60 days and then exclude or void claims. Ask what endorsement they need and by when.

**Keep the utilities on.** Do not cancel and do not transfer them yet. In winter, keep the heat at 55°F or above.

**Photograph everything.** Every room, drawer, the garage, the safe, the jewelry. Date-stamped, before anyone else visits. This is your inventory baseline and your protection against "where did Dad's watch go."

**Lock away the sensitive things.** Cash, jewelry, firearms, medication, documents. Log what you moved and where.

**Locate the documents.** The will and trust. The last two tax returns. Bank, brokerage and retirement statements. Insurance policies. Deeds, titles, mortgage statements. Marriage certificate, divorce decree, military discharge papers. The safe-deposit box key. The password list.

## Weeks two to four: do you need probate, and getting appointed

Being named in a will gives you no authority. The court gives you authority, in the form of a document called Letters Testamentary (with a will) or Letters of Administration (without). Until you hold Letters, banks will not talk to you.

Before filing anything, sort the assets into three piles:

- **A. Passes by beneficiary designation:** life insurance, IRAs, 401(k)s, annuities, payable-on-death accounts. Not probate.
- **B. Passes by ownership:** joint accounts, a house owned with a surviving spouse, anything held in a living trust. Not probate.
- **C. Everything else:** accounts and property in your parent's name alone. This is the probate estate.

Add up pile C at date-of-death values. Every state has a small-estate shortcut, with limits from roughly $15,000 to over $200,000, that skips most of formal probate. Call the probate clerk in the county where your parent lived and ask what this year's limit is and which form applies. Clerks answer this every day.

If pile C is over the limit, file the petition, get appointed, and the day you receive Letters do four things: get an EIN for the estate at irs.gov (free), open an estate checking account, file IRS Form 56, and go to the post office with your Letters to forward the mail.

## Days 30 to 90: where the deadlines live

Publish the notice to creditors on your state's schedule. Mail written notice to every known creditor. File the inventory with the court by its deadline. Get written date-of-death values for everything, including a licensed appraisal of the house. Get a CPA involved before the final tax return is due in April.

And send the beneficiaries a written update on the same day every month, whether or not anything changed. Silence is what starts family fights, not the will.
