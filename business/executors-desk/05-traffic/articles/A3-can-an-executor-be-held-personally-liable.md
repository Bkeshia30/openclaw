# Can an executor be held personally liable? Yes, in five specific ways

You have seen the phrase on a law firm's website, probably late at night. It is true. It is also narrower than the websites make it sound, and every case fits one of five patterns.

## First, what "fiduciary" means

An executor is a fiduciary: someone legally required to act in another person's interest, here the beneficiaries and, until they are paid, the creditors. Breach that duty in a way that costs the estate money, and a court can order you to make the estate whole from your own funds. Beneficiaries bring these claims. Creditors occasionally do. The state almost never does.

## 1. Paying people too early

A sibling wants "their share." A cousin wants the car. A beneficiary wants a check. Distribute before the creditor claim window closes and the taxes are paid or reserved for, and if a valid claim or tax bill shows up afterward, the estate may have nothing left to pay it. That gap can be yours.

**The habit:** nothing goes to beneficiaries until the window has closed and the CPA has signed off. Say "I am not allowed to yet, and here is the date" as often as needed.

## 2. Mixing money

Depositing an estate check into your own account, even briefly, even to "keep it safe." Paying an estate bill from your own card without a receipt and a log entry. Commingling looks like theft on a bank statement even when it was not, and it is the single most common allegation in executor lawsuits.

**The habit:** the day you receive Letters, get an EIN and open an estate account. Everything runs through it. Your reimbursements come out of it, against receipts.

## 3. Missing a deadline you did not know existed

Lodging the will with the court (often 30 days). Publishing notice to creditors. Filing the inventory. The final income tax return. The estate income tax return. Each has a clock. Miss the creditor-notice deadline and the estate may lose the right to reject a late claim. Miss a tax deadline and there are penalties, and beneficiaries can look to you for them.

**The habit:** call the probate clerk the week you are appointed and write every date they give you on one calendar.

## 4. Spending a benefit overpayment

Social Security pays in arrears and pays nothing for the month of death. A deposit that lands after the death will be reclaimed. Pensions and annuities do the same. If the money has been spent, you replace it.

**The habit:** leave every post-death deposit untouched until the payer confirms in writing what it wants back.

## 5. Playing favorites

Paying the creditor who shouts loudest when the estate cannot pay everyone. Selling the house to a friend below market. Letting one sibling clear out the garage before the inventory is done. Executors owe every beneficiary the same duty of impartiality, and every creditor the order of priority set by state law.

**The habit:** document every decision, get a written valuation for anything of value, and when family is involved, announce the method for dividing personal items in writing before anyone chooses.

## The one-sentence test

Before any money moves: *if a beneficiary saw this on a bank statement a year from now, would it look fair, documented, and in order?* If not, do not do it yet.

## When to get a lawyer involved

If the estate may be insolvent, if there is property in more than one state, if anyone is threatening a contest, if there is a business or rental property, or if the estate is large enough for estate tax. For a modest, solvent estate with a clear will and cooperative heirs, a one-hour limited-scope consultation to review your plan is usually the best value; bring your inventory and your deadline calendar.
